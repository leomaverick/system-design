Microservices & Distributed Transactions

1. Saga Pattern

A Saga manages a distributed business transaction as a series of local transactions performed by independently owned services. Each service commits its own database changes; there is no single shared ACID transaction spanning all services.

A Saga defines both the successful workflow and compensating actions to recover business consistency if later steps fail. It is not merely an exception handler.

2. Two-Service Happy Path

A customer buys an item for $100 using two microservices, each with its own database.

  1. Order Service: creates an order with status PENDING and commits.
  2. Payment Service: processes the $100 payment and commits.
  3. Order Service: receives confirmation and changes the order status to CONFIRMED.

Result: Order Created → Payment Succeeded → Order Confirmed. No compensation is necessary because every step succeeded.

3. Compensating Transactions

Suppose Payment fails after Order has already committed. The Saga can direct Order to mark the pending order CANCELLED. In a longer workflow, if Payment succeeds but Inventory cannot reserve the product, a compensation may include refunding Payment and cancelling Order.

Compensation is not a database rollback. A refund is a new business operation; it does not erase the original payment record. Compensations may themselves fail and require durable tracking, safe retries, and operational recovery.

4. Consistency and Transaction Boundaries

A traditional ACID transaction can roll back its own uncommitted work within its transaction scope. A Saga instead coordinates several committed local transactions across service boundaries. As a result, intermediate business states (such as PENDING) may be visible before the overall workflow completes.

The design aims to maintain business consistency, often through eventual consistency, rather than providing the atomicity and isolation of one global database transaction.

5. Coordination vs. Compensation

Coordination determines which local action happens next and how completion or failure is tracked. Compensation defines corrective business actions when earlier successful steps must be reversed in effect.

Both belong to the Saga Pattern: the success path coordinates normal work; the failure path coordinates compensation.

6. Saga Orchestration

A central orchestrator controls the workflow, tracks progress, issues commands to services, and decides when compensations are needed. It can communicate over REST, messaging, or a combination.

  1. Orchestrator requests CreateOrder; Order commits PENDING.
  2. Orchestrator requests ProcessPayment; Payment completes successfully.
  3. Orchestrator requests ConfirmOrder.

If Payment fails, the orchestrator requests CancelOrder instead. Advantages include visibility and a clear control flow; trade-offs include extra infrastructure and centralized workflow coupling. Production designs should persist Saga state and handle duplicate commands and recovery.

7. Saga Choreography

With choreography, there is no central workflow controller. Services react to business events from other services.

  1. Order creates a pending order and publishes OrderCreated.
  2. Payment handles the event, processes payment, then publishes PaymentSucceeded or PaymentFailed.
  3. Order reacts to the payment event by confirming or cancelling the order.

This is typically event-driven. Kafka or RabbitMQ can carry events, but the specific broker is not what defines the pattern. Choreography can reduce central coordination dependencies but make end-to-end flows harder to trace and evolve.

8. REST vs. Events

Saga does not require Event-Driven Architecture. An orchestrator can coordinate through synchronous REST calls, asynchronous messages, or both. Choreography is usually event-driven because services react to published business events.

REST: direct request-response. It is convenient when the caller needs an immediate answer, but it introduces a synchronous dependency: a slow Payment Service may delay Order unless timeouts and resilience mechanisms are used.

Events/messages: Order publishes OrderCreated, Payment consumes it independently and publishes PaymentSucceeded, and Order then updates its state. Messaging reduces runtime coupling and supports asynchronous workflows, but adds delivery, ordering, observability, and duplicate-handling challenges.

Both styles are widely used together in retail; events are not universally the best choice for every interaction.

Key distinction: orchestration versus choreography describes who controls the workflow, not which transport is used.

9. Eventual Consistency

Eventual consistency allows different services to temporarily reflect different states while updates propagate, with the expectation that they converge when communication and processing succeed. It often occurs in Saga workflows, but is not exclusive to Saga.

Retail example: Order commits PENDING; Payment charges $100 and commits. Before Order receives the PaymentSucceeded event (or a follow-up response), Payment shows success while Order still shows PENDING. After the update reaches Order, it changes to CONFIRMED.

Convergence needs reliable communication and handling of failures: retries, idempotent consumers, and reconciliation when messages are delayed or lost. Eventual consistency is not a guarantee that an unreliable workflow will fix itself automatically.

10. Atomic Inventory Reservations

For a marketplace selling a one-of-a-kind handbag, two buyers may attempt to reserve the same available item at once. The Inventory Service must protect this local invariant even when requests arrive at separate service instances.

UPDATE inventory
SET status = 'RESERVED'
WHERE product_id = 123
  AND status = 'AVAILABLE';

Execute the conditional update within an appropriate database transaction and inspect the affected row count. One updated row means the reservation succeeded; zero means the item was not available. PostgreSQL coordinates concurrent updates with row-level locking, so two concurrent conditional updates cannot both transition the same row from AVAILABLE to RESERVED. This is database-level concurrency control, not a distinction between synchronous and asynchronous messaging.

No compensation for the losing buyer: if Buyer B's reservation updates zero rows, no reservation was committed to undo. But if Buyer A successfully reserves and a later payment step fails, the Saga compensates by releasing that reservation. Use a reservation identifier and safe state checks so a delayed release cannot accidentally free an item reserved by somebody else.

Separation of concerns: atomic local operations enforce inventory correctness; Saga coordinates transactions across Order, Inventory, and Payment. A local conditional update needs no global Saga or distributed lock just to prevent double reservation.

11. Interview-Ready Answers

What is the Saga Pattern?

“The Saga Pattern manages a distributed business transaction through a sequence of local transactions across microservices. It coordinates the normal workflow and uses compensating operations when later steps fail, without requiring one global database transaction.”

Compensation vs. Rollback

“A database rollback undoes uncommitted work within its transaction. A Saga compensation is a new business operation, such as refunding a completed payment or cancelling an order, to reverse earlier business effects.”

Orchestration vs. Choreography

“Orchestration has a central coordinator that controls the Saga's progress and can use REST or events. Choreography has no central coordinator; services usually coordinate by publishing and consuming business events.”

Eventual consistency in retail

“Order and Payment can temporarily show different states while an event or response propagates. With reliable delivery and processing, the order converges from PENDING to CONFIRMED.”

Preventing overselling

“I use a conditional atomic database update and check the affected row count to ensure only one reservation succeeds, then use Saga compensation to release the reservation if a later payment fails.”

REST vs. events

“REST supports immediate request-response; event-driven messaging supports asynchronous, loosely coupled workflows. A retail platform can combine both depending on business needs.”
